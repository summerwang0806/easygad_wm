task; it is our baseline throughout. Its limits were not previously characterized, and establishing them
is part of our contribution. The statistic accumulates evidence only where exact watermarked contexts
recur, which we show is fragile to wider windows, lower decoding temperatures, and larger text budgets
(§3.2, App. C.5), the same failure mode that paraphrase attacks exploit against surface-level watermark
detection (Sadasivan et al., 2023). WM-Basin keeps the access level and the generations, and replaces
the token statistic with a weight-space one.
Weight-space geometry and basins.WM-Basin relies on the fact that students distilled under one key
settle into a shared, key-specific region of weight space. This connects to the geometry of neural loss
landscapes: independently trained optima are joined by simple, near-constant-loss curves (Garipov
et al., 2018), and once permutation symmetries are accounted for, the landscape resembles a sin-
gle widebasin, so separately trained models can be aligned by permutation and merged in weight
space (Ainsworth et al., 2022). Large language models exhibit the same basin-like structure (Chen
et al., 2026; Li et al., 2018; Peng et al., 2024). WM-Basin treats the watermark key as carving out one
such basin and asks whether a suspect has fallen into it.
Gradient attribution and representation post-processing.To read this geometric signal from text alone,
WM-Basin backpropagates a suspect’s completions through a fixed probe model. This draws on first-
order data attribution: influence functions trace a prediction back to the training points responsible
for it using only gradient access (Koh and Liang, 2017), an idea scaled by gradient tracing (Pruthi
et al., 2020) and random-projected attribution (Park et al., 2023), and adapted to instruction tuning
with low-rank projected gradients (Xia et al., 2024). Intuitively, a training example’s gradient captures
how it would nudge the model, so aligned gradients indicate similar examples. Raw gradient cosines,
however, are dominated by a few shared directions (generic language and formatting): a handful
of top components inflate the similarity between otherwise unrelated items (Ethayarajh, 2019; Gao
et al., 2019; Radovanovic et al., 2010; Dinu et al., 2014). We remove these with an “All-but-the-Top”
projection (Mu et al., 2017), stripping the few dominant yet uninformative directions so that the faint
key-specific signal stands out from the generic task geometry.
5 Conclusion and Future Work
In this work, we introduce thewatermark basin: distilling on watermarked traces encodes the teacher’s
secret key as a faint direction in parameter space, displacing the student’s weights along that key
direction and into a region that later training does not easily escape. Building on this geometry, we
design WM-Basin, which reformulates black-box detection of unauthorized distillation from a fragile,
surface-level token count into a geometric alignment test in parameter space. Across a wide range of
evaluations, spanning six student families, four teachers, and four watermarking schemes, WM-Basin
outperforms the existing baseline by a large margin, and it survives quantization, pruning, and RL
post-training.
Scope and outlook.A watermark can be present in a distilled model but unreadable in its text:
continued SFT on clean data suppresses the text-level signal while an open-weight probe still recovers
the residual, and a supplementary study (App. D.3) finds the same for rewriting the teacher’s traces
before distillation. WM-Basin is also an in-domain test: a hit tells the verifier on which domain the
suspect’s text carries the watermark, not only that it carries one, and a low score elsewhere means
nothing was found there, not that the model is clean (§3.2). Both are limits on what a black-box
verifier can observe, not on what distillation transfers. Narrowing the gap between the black-box read
and the open-weight probe is thus a promising direction for future work.

model was distilled from its watermarked teacher. It involves no human subjects and no personal or
user data: the distillation corpus (OpenR1-Math-220k), the utility benchmarks (GSM8K and Math-
500), and every teacher, student, probe, and clean-pool checkpoint are publicly released artefacts used
