1 Introduction
Proprietary language models are increasingly targeted by API-based distillation: an adversary queries
a closed teacher model and fine-tunes a competing student model on its outputs. This attack extends
black-box model extraction, first demonstrated against ML-as-a-service systems (Tramèr et al., 2016),
and has become a major threat to reasoning LLMs (Anthropic, 2026a; Google Threat Intelligence
Group, 2026; Remler and Hayum, 2026). Providers have responded by withholding reasoning traces,
but concealment does not prevent distillation. Hidden traces can be extracted at scale from encrypted
response blocks (Panfilov et al., 2026), while answers and short summaries can be expanded into
synthetic traces that recover much of the benefit of training on the originals (Zhang et al., 2026). Other
defenses make extraction harder rather than detectable (Anthropic, 2026a; Google Threat Intelligence
Group, 2026; Savani et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026). We review related work in full in §4.
In this work, we focus on output watermarking, which is encouraged by emerging regulatory require-
ments (European Commission, 2026). Anthropic, for example, embeds watermarks in texts generated
by Claude models released beginning in August 2026 (Anthropic, 2026b). Such watermarks can survive
distillation: Sander et al. (2024) show that students trained on watermarked teacher outputs retain a
“radioactive” signal. Their black-box detector, to our knowledge the only existing one for this setting
and our baseline throughout, tests suspect outputs for an excess of watermark-favored tokens, but only
where watermark-conditioned contexts recur. We find that this dependence is fragile: detection de-
grades sharply with larger context windows and loses most of its power under low-temperature teacher
sampling.We therefore seek a watermark signal that does not require exact token-level recurrence.
Our main finding is thewatermark basin: supervised fine-tuning on clean (watermark-free) teacher
traces moves a student along a broadtask direction, whereas watermarked traces add a faint, key-
specificwatermark direction(Fig. 1), leading the student into a key-specific region of parameter space.
Post-training updates cannot easily push the student out of this basin. Building on this geometry,
we introduceWatermark Basin Alignment (WM-Basin), a black-box detector that represents the suspect
model’s outputs and the watermarked teacher’s outputs as gradients of one fixed, clean probe model.
After removing the task subspace estimated from clean models, the distilled student should align with
the watermark keymore closelythan with decoy keys that were never used to watermark the teacher.
WM-Basin turns this relative rank into acalibratedp-value usinga non-parametric ranking test (Dwass,
1957): a small value is evidence that the suspect was distilled from the claimed teacher and key on the
audited domain (Fig. 2).
